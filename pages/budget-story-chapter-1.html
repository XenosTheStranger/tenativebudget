Why does the County need a budget?

A budget is required to guide how public funds are used. Each year, Walton County estimates revenues, identifies service needs, and determines what can be funded. The budget brings these decisions together into a single public plan that is reviewed before any spending occurs.

What are the major challenges facing the organization?

Six named conditions shaped this proposal.

Holding the millage rate at the rolled-back rate limits new revenue from the existing tax base even as costs continue to change. The Sheriff’s budget draws a General Fund transfer that competes with other County departments. Recurring costs like personnel and contracts have to be kept aligned with recurring revenue, not covered with one-time money. And fund balance is not one open pool — much of it is legally restricted or Board-committed to a specific purpose.

What are the major challenges facing the community?

Growth and tourism drive real, ongoing needs.

Roads, drainage, public facilities, and recreation assets have to keep pace with a growing population, which is why the County plans capital projects on a five-year horizon rather than one year at a time. At the same time, Tourist Development Taxes, fuel taxes, grants, and impact fees bring in outside money, but each comes with limits on how and where it can be spent, and collections can be harder to predict than property taxes.

How does this budget address those challenges?

Each challenge has a specific, budgeted response.

Departments went through a required budget-reduction exercise so trade-offs could be weighed against the rolled-back millage rate. The five-year Capital Improvement Plan sequences infrastructure projects by funding source and timing. The five-year financial forecast tests whether recurring revenue keeps pace with recurring costs through FY 2031. And the Board-committed Tourist Development balance for Beach Renourishment is tracked separately from money available for other eligible uses, so restricted dollars aren’t mistaken for flexible ones.

Are results expected to improve within the budget period?

Progress is tracked, not just assumed.

Success is measured against specific markers: whether the budget adopted is balanced, whether recurring revenue keeps pace with recurring expenditures, how projected fund balance trends over the forecast period, and whether capital projects hit their planned timing and funding. These are checked on an ongoing basis, not declared solved in a single year.

What policies or regulations guide the budget?

Florida Statutes, plus the County’s own adopted policies.

The Budget Policy is written per Florida Statutes Chapters 129 and 200. On top of that legal floor, the County has adopted its own Fund Balance Policy, which sets reserve targets for financial stability and credit rating, and a Budget Transfers and Amendments policy, which sets the procedure for changing the budget once it’s adopted.

Is the County legally required to adopt a budget?

Yes — it isn’t optional.

Florida law requires an annual budget to be prepared, approved, and adopted for every fiscal year, following Chapters 125, 129, 200, and 218 of the Florida Statutes. For Walton County that process runs from roughly February through September, ending with legal adoption of the millage rate and the budget at a formally noticed public hearing.

Can the County spend money without an adopted budget?

No. Spending is capped at the fund level.

Budgetary control is established at the fund level for every fund, so total appropriations can’t exceed what was approved. If circumstances change — an unforeseen cost, a new grant, unanticipated revenue — the Board has to formally amend the budget by resolution under Section 129.06, Florida Statutes, before that spending can happen.

Who is responsible for putting the budget together?

OMB coordinates it; many people build it.

The Office of Management and Budget issues the written instructions that open the process and sets the cost assumptions every department has to use, so requests are comparable. From there, departments submit requests, County Administration reviews them, independently elected Constitutional Officers submit their own budgets under Florida law, and the five-member Board of County Commissioners makes the final decisions in public meetings.
